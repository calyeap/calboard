Profile: pre-revenue / unprofitable — owner-operator infrastructure, recommended by software, confirmed by analyst. The validated scope of this profile is narrower than “pre-revenue”: it assumes capex per unit of capacity, a capacity ramp, a construction lead and a unit-economics breakeven.

On two of the six ways this company could succeed, shareholders would end up with less than if it simply shut down and returned its cash.

Why it matters
For a company with no revenue, the usual question is "how likely is success?" That question is meaningless until you say which success. Building the reactors but earning a thin return on them, after raising the money it takes to build them, can leave existing shareholders worse off than the cash on the balance sheet today. That is not a modelling error — it is the single most useful thing this analysis produced.
For Oklo
The cash the company holds today is worth about $3.10 per share. Two of the six success definitions produce less than that once the dilution needed to fund construction is counted. Calboard refuses to compute an implied probability for those two, because the arithmetic that converts price into a probability has no meaning when success is worth less than failure.
What to examine
What separates the two failing definitions from the ones that work — the tariff assumed, and how quickly capacity comes online. Then how much new equity each case requires, and on what terms.
Why six definitions instead of one probability
A single "probability of success" figure requires a single definition of success, and for a pre-revenue infrastructure company there isn't one. Deploying early at a low tariff and deploying 8 GW at utility economics are different outcomes with different values — one of them can be worth less than the cash floor while the other is worth ten times it. Calboard shows the value of each definition and only computes a probability where the definition is worth more than failure.

D — Implied probability of success

Per definition · never one number

Each row asks the same question: if this particular version of success happened, what would a share be worth — and is that more than the cash the company already holds?

Why it matters
Implied probability works by comparing today's price against two outcomes: what a share is worth if the company succeeds, and what it is worth if it fails. When the success value falls below the failure value, that comparison inverts and the answer becomes nonsense. Showing a number anyway would be worse than showing nothing.
For Oklo
Rows are ordered by success value, lowest first, so the two that fall below the cash floor appear at the top rather than buried at the bottom. Three of the six also hit the discount-rate cap, which means those values are upper bounds — the true figure is lower, not higher.
What to examine
The spread between definitions 3 and 4. They differ only in how quickly capacity comes online, and that alone moves the value from $31 to $48 a share. That gap is the honest uncertainty in this analysis.
Why two different discount rates in the same row
Success and failure carry different risk, so they are discounted at different rates — and both are shown. What must match across the two is the basis: same date, same share count, same dilution treatment. Same basis does not mean same rate. Where the success rate hits the 30% cap, the value shown is an upper bound and is flagged as one.

V_fail and V_success are on the same basis — same date, same share base, same dilution treatment. Each is discounted at the rate appropriate to its own risk, and both rates are displayed. Same basis does not mean the same discount rate. Rows are ordered by success value ascending.

D — Funding stack

Four lines · solved year by year · both ramps

Building reactors costs more than this company has, so the gap gets filled — and the order it gets filled in decides how much of the upside existing shareholders keep.

Why it matters
Every dollar raised by issuing new shares splits the future between more owners. A model that assumes cheap debt arrives first and equity only as a last resort will flatter the outcome. The order of the funding stack is an assumption, and it is worth as much as any growth rate.
For Oklo
Calboard solves this year by year rather than in aggregate. Solved in aggregate, cash from the last reactors pays to build the first ones — an impossibility worth about $27 per share on the 8 GW case. New equity is the residual, raised only in years the cash balance would otherwise go negative.
What to examine
The dilution figure for each ramp, and the caveats below it. The model raises equity only when forced; real companies raise ahead of need and usually on worse terms.

H — Fair-value range

Distribution summary · not bear/bull bounds

There is no single fair value here, and compressing this into a bear-to-bull range would hide the only thing worth knowing — that the outcomes are not on a continuum.

Why it matters
For a profitable company, value varies smoothly with assumptions, so a range makes sense. For a pre-revenue company the outcomes are closer to separate worlds: the reactors get built and earn a return, or they do not and what remains is cash. Averaging across those produces a number that describes neither.
For Oklo
So the output is a distribution summary instead: what failure is worth, what success is worth as commonly described, and what success would have to look like to justify today's price. The cash floor sits underneath as the one figure that does not depend on the reactors working.
What to examine
The distance between "success as commonly described" and "success as the price requires". That gap is the question the price is really asking.
