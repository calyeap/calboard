Calboard could not produce a meaningful valuation at the higher discount rates, and it is refusing to show a number rather than showing a misleading one.

Why it matters
A discounted-cash-flow model splits value into a forecast period and everything after it, called the terminal period. When almost all the value lands in that distant tail, the answer stops being a valuation and starts being an assumption about the far future wearing a valuation's clothes.
For Microsoft
Nothing about the company drives this. The return Microsoft earns on new investment is higher than the rate used to discount it, so at 12% the model's value grows faster than it is discounted away and the arithmetic stops converging. Four of the nine reverse-DCF cells break this way.
What to examine
The cells that do resolve, and how much of their value sits in the terminal period. Whether the current operating margin — a high point for this company — is the right level to be projecting from.
What is a discount rate?
The rate used to convert future cash into today's money. A higher rate means future cash is worth less today, so it produces a lower valuation and demands more growth to justify a given price. Calboard runs every company at 8%, 10% and 12% from policy configuration — the rate is never chosen per company and never chosen by the interpretation layer.

D — Deterministic diagnostics

M1–M14 · extract shown

Microsoft is earning a high return on the money it reinvests, but it is currently doing so at the best profit margin in its own ten-year record.

Why it matters
A company that reinvests heavily and earns a good return on that money compounds value. The risk is projecting forward from a peak: if today's margin is the best the business has ever managed, using it as the base case quietly assumes the best year is the new normal.
For Microsoft
The current margin is the highest in the available window, and that window is wide. Calboard flags this as a description of where the margin sits — not as a claim that the business is cyclical. The separate cyclicality test did not fire, and no revenue decline is asserted anywhere as a result.
What to examine
The gap between the current margin and the ten-year median, and what a valuation looks like at the median instead of the peak. Both are run below.
Why one flag fired and the other did not
Two tests are evaluated separately because they make different claims. Margin at historical high is a description: the current margin is at or near the window maximum and the window is wide. Cyclical is a claim about the business: it requires an actual single-year margin collapse in the record. Microsoft fires the first and not the second. A software company whose margin rose steadily while scaling is not cyclical, and asserting a revenue decline in its bear case on that evidence would be a fabrication.

E — Price-implied diagnostics

Diagnostic reverse DCF · 3 margin levels × 3 rates

Today's share price already assumes Microsoft grows revenue about 18.5% a year for five years — which sounds punishing, but works out to roughly 13.7% a year over ten, close to what the company has actually done for the last decade.

Why it matters
This is the question the price is asking, run backwards. Rather than guessing what a company is worth, it solves for the growth the current price already requires. That turns a valuation into something you can check against the company's own record.
For Microsoft
The five-year figure and the ten-year figure tell different stories, which is why both are always shown. 18.5% for five years reads as demanding. The same path expressed over ten years is 13.7%, sitting at the low end of Microsoft's own 13.8–14.7% history rather than above it. A large share of today's value also depends on growth that has not happened yet — earnings the company has not generated.
What to examine
Whether that growth rate is still available at Microsoft's current size, and whether the returns on reinvested capital that make it valuable can hold. Four cells refuse to resolve at all — that pattern, concentrated at high rates and low margins, is itself information.
What is a reverse DCF, and why nine cells?
A normal discounted-cash-flow model takes your growth assumption and produces a value. A reverse DCF takes the market's value — the price — and produces the growth assumption it implies. It removes the step where the analyst's own optimism enters the arithmetic. Nine cells because two things must be varied that materially change the answer: the discount rate (8%, 10%, 12%) and the profit margin being projected (current, ten-year median, a stress level). One cell would hide how sensitive the answer is to both.
See calculation — PVGO
Steady-state EV = median-margin NOPAT ÷ discount rate. PVGO = current EV − steady-state EV, EV to EV. The PVGO share is that difference over current EV. Because trigger A fired, PVGO is also shown on current NOPAT beside median-margin NOPAT, and the gap between them is printed — a margin at its own historical high makes the two materially different.
What is PVGO?
Present Value of Growth Opportunities. Split the company's value in two: what it would be worth if it simply carried on at its current profit level forever, and everything above that. The second part is PVGO — the share of today's price that is a bet on growth that has not happened yet. A high share is not a warning by itself; it means the valuation is more sensitive to whether growth and returns on new investment hold up.

Every cell reports years 1–5 growth and the equivalent ten-year CAGR and year-10 revenue. The five-year figure alone reads as far more demanding than the same path actually is. RONIC is printed beside every implied-growth figure, with its state. Four of the nine cells return a state rather than a number.

H — Fair-value range

Never shown alone

This is a range, not a target, and it is deliberately shown side by side with what the price already assumes — so neither your view nor the market's is read on its own.

Why it matters
A single fair-value number invites you to compare it to the price and act. A range next to the market's own implied assumptions invites a different question: where do my assumptions actually differ from what is already priced in, and why?
For Microsoft
The bounds are the bear and bull cases as authored, not a statistical confidence interval — and because the margin-at-high flag fired, they carry that warning explicitly. The weighted figure sits inside the range and is never the headline.
What to examine
Where the current price falls in the range, and the discount rate at which the base case equals the price — both shown in section G. If that rate is far from the policy band, that gap is the disagreement.

I — Interpretation

I2 — Challenger findings

A separate call that did not see the analysis

Findings are placed alongside the analysis, not reconciled with it. Neither side is rewritten in light of the other. Each finding shows the claim or fact it bears on, its evidence, and what would have to be true for it to matter. One call, one set of findings — no personas, no voting, no scoring.
